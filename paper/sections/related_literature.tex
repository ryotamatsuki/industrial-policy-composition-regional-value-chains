\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The paper relates to four strands of work. First, it is part of the broad fiscal-federalism literature on strategic local policy choice and interjurisdictional spillovers. The survey by \citet{AgrawalHoytWilson2022} emphasizes expenditure spillovers, fiscal competition, bidding for firms, and constraints imposed by higher-level governments as central sources of local-policy interaction. More specifically, \citet{CremerMarchandPestieau1997} study two communities that make indivisible local-public-service investments with interjurisdictional spillovers and compare Nash provision with a social optimum. \citet{BlochZenginobuz2007} characterize local-public-good provision with positive spillovers, including uniqueness and multiplicity as spillovers vary, while \citet{Takahashi2004} studies strategic local-government investment in public facilities and coordination failure. These papers make clear that incomplete internalization of cross-jurisdiction benefits and a wedge between decentralized and coordinated decisions are not new mechanisms by themselves. The present paper instead asks how that familiar fiscal-externality logic operates on a different margin: the composition of a fixed industrial-policy portfolio across vertically complementary activities.

Second, a growing industrial-policy literature studies strategic policy across jurisdictions and sectors. \citet{ChenLi2026} develop a quantitative spatial-equilibrium model of local industrial subsidy competition and compare local, cooperative, and central policy under varying market integration. \citet{WangXuYangZhu2024} solve non-cooperative tariffs and sector-specific industrial subsidies across many economies and sectors. These contributions already establish that industrial policy can be strategic and multi-sectoral. Our object is narrower: each government has a fixed total policy capacity and chooses its composition across activities, so the distortion can be isolated while the policy total is held constant across decentralized and coordinated regimes.

Third, related work studies the composition of public expenditure and the allocation of intervention across stages of production. \citet{KikuchiKuzawaTamai2026} analyze public-expenditure composition under fiscal competition, where employment, tax-financing, and fiscal externalities can distort the mix of public goods and public inputs. In green and innovation policy, \citet{Fischer2017} examines strategic green industrial policy, \citet{FischerGreakerRosendahl2018} distinguish upstream technology support from downstream deployment support, and \citet{BorotaMilicevicEtAl2026} analyze cooperation in regional R\&D subsidies with cross-border knowledge diffusion. The present paper does not claim novelty for expenditure composition, upstream/downstream policy, or coordination per se. Its narrower object combines a binding portfolio constraint with cross-regional complementarity between different activities and derives an exact interval in which decentralized priority duplication is unique although coordinated reallocation of the same capacity is already preferred.

Fourth, the paper connects to place-based policy and smart specialization. Smart-specialization strategies explicitly require regions to concentrate policy on a limited set of priorities and related transformative activities \citep{ForayEichlerKeller2021}. Recent evidence from China shows that local governments systematically favor industries aligned with their own specialization and do not display the same favoritism toward supply-chain-linked industries or specialization extending across jurisdictional boundaries \citep{TianWangZhang2027}. These observations motivate the model's combination of scarce policy capacity, a local return premium, and cross-jurisdiction complementarity. They do not identify the model parameter values or establish the welfare result; those remain theoretical objects.

The surviving contribution should therefore be read narrowly. It is not the general proposition that spillovers are under-internalized, that local governments compete, that industrial policy can duplicate activity, or that coordination can improve welfare. It is the fixed-capacity industrial-policy portfolio game and its composition-specific equilibrium characterization: incomplete local capture of complementary cross-regional value can leave both governments uniquely concentrated on the locally attractive activity over a positive-measure interval after coordinated reallocation of the same policy capacity has become welfare improving.
