\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Regional industrial policy is often described as a question of how much support a government should provide to favored sectors. Yet many practical programs also force a different choice: how a limited policy capacity should be divided across competing priorities. This compositional margin matters when activities are complementary across regional borders. A region may prefer the activity that generates the highest local return, even when the national economy would gain more if that region instead supported an activity that complements production elsewhere.

This paper studies a simple version of that problem. Two regional governments allocate a fixed policy capacity between a locally high-return activity and a complementary activity. Cross-regional matches between the two activities generate additional value. The central friction is incidence: the region that undertakes the complementary activity captures only part of the total cross-regional value it creates. The question is whether decentralized governments reallocate policy toward complementarity at the same point at which coordinated reallocation becomes welfare improving.

The answer is no. In the baseline continuous portfolio game, the coordinated fixed-capacity planner changes policy composition when cross-regional complementarity reaches
\[
A^P=\Delta,
\]
where $\Delta$ is the direct local surplus premium from the locally attractive activity. The decentralized equilibrium set changes at
\[
A^N=\frac{\Delta}{1-\alpha},
\]
where $1-\alpha$ is the share of complementary value captured by the region undertaking the complementary activity. Since $0<\alpha<1$, $A^P<A^N$. Consequently, for the positive-measure interval
\[
\Delta<A<\frac{\Delta}{1-\alpha},
\]
complete priority duplication is the unique Nash equilibrium even though a coordinated reallocation of the same total policy capacity is strictly welfare improving.

The result is a composition-specific decentralization failure. It is not driven by decentralized governments spending more in total: each region has the same fixed policy capacity under decentralization and coordination. Nor is it an equilibrium-selection result in the intermediate region: the inefficient duplicated profile is the unique Nash equilibrium. The difference lies in which activities receive support. A coordinator values the full complementary surplus, while the jurisdiction that must give up the locally attractive activity internalizes only its own share.

The incidence logic itself is familiar from fiscal federalism and local-public-good theory: cross-jurisdiction benefits need not be fully internalized by the government taking the costly action. Classic work already compares decentralized and socially coordinated local investments under interjurisdictional spillovers \citep{CremerMarchandPestieau1997,BlochZenginobuz2007,Takahashi2004}, and the broader local-policy literature treats expenditure spillovers as a standard source of strategic interaction \citep{AgrawalHoytWilson2022}. The paper's contribution is therefore not a new generic externality theorem. It is to place that logic on the fixed-composition margin of industrial policy and derive the complete portfolio equilibrium: the model identifies an exact positive-measure interval in which both governments uniquely retain the same locally attractive priority even though coordination has already switched the composition of the same policy capacity.

The fixed-capacity environment is maintained rather than derived. Its purpose is to hold the total policy resource constant and isolate priority composition from the familiar question of over- or under-spending. This assumption is institutionally motivated by programs in which governments must concentrate limited administrative, fiscal, or organizational capacity on a subset of priorities, but the paper does not claim that actual budgets are literally fixed or that the same result must survive endogenous total spending.

The baseline is deliberately spare, but the threshold logic is not tied to Cobb--Douglas production. A binary switching representation shows that if a reallocation sacrifices local surplus $\Delta$, creates total complementary value $G$, and the reallocating jurisdiction captures a fraction $\lambda\in(0,1)$, then the coordinated threshold is $G^P=\Delta$ whereas the local threshold is $G^N=\Delta/\lambda$. A restricted concave constant-returns production class yields the same incidence ordering, and an alternative capacity-matching technology preserves reallocation away from duplication. The latter exercise also identifies an important limit: coordination need not imply complete regional specialization. The robust claim is about delayed decentralized portfolio adjustment, not about a universal geographic pattern.

The analysis speaks to current debates over place-based industrial policy and regional priority setting. Smart-specialization policy explicitly organizes public support around a limited set of regional priorities \citep{ForayEichlerKeller2021}, while the European Union's Interregional Innovation Investments program supports investments based on shared or complementary regional priorities to strengthen value chains \citep{EISMEA2026}. The OECD similarly emphasizes alignment of industrial priorities with local capabilities and coordination across levels of government \citep{OECD2025}. Empirically, \citet{TianWangZhang2027} find that Chinese local governments favor industries aligned with their own specialization and do not show the same favoritism toward supply-chain-linked activities or specialization extending across jurisdictional boundaries. These facts motivate the ingredients of the model without being used to calibrate it.

The paper is also distinct from existing models of strategic industrial-policy levels and public-expenditure composition. Quantitative work already studies local industrial subsidy competition and central versus decentralized policy \citep{ChenLi2026}, and multi-country models allow governments to choose sector-specific subsidies strategically \citep{WangXuYangZhu2024}. Related work separates upstream and downstream interventions \citep{Fischer2017,FischerGreakerRosendahl2018}, studies cross-border coordination of innovation subsidies \citep{BorotaMilicevicEtAl2026}, and shows that fiscal competition can distort the composition of public expenditure through tax and employment externalities \citep{KikuchiKuzawaTamai2026}. The surviving contribution here is narrower: fixing overall policy capacity makes priority composition itself the strategic object, while cross-regional complementarity between different activities generates the exact duplication-versus-reallocation interval characterized above.

The policy implication is correspondingly limited. The paper does not show that centralization is optimal or that every region should specialize in a different activity. Administrative costs, local information advantages, endogenous total policy budgets, transferable surplus across jurisdictions, and endogenous intergovernmental institutions are outside the model. The result is that, within the fixed-capacity environment, coordination of priority choices can improve the allocation of modeled real surplus even when the total amount of support does not change.
