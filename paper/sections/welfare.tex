\section{Coordinated Benchmark and Welfare}
\label{sec:welfare}

The coordinated benchmark chooses $(x_1,x_2)\in[0,1]^2$ to maximize the sum of regional payoffs while holding fixed each region's total policy capacity, the policy instruments, information, production technology, and matching technology. It is therefore a \emph{coordinated fixed-capacity planner benchmark}, not an unrestricted first best.

Summing \eqref{eq:local-payoff} across regions gives
\begin{align}
W(x_1,x_2)
&\equiv W_1(x_1,x_2)+W_2(x_2,x_1)\\
&=2b_D+\Delta(x_1+x_2)
+A\left[x_1(1-x_2)+(1-x_1)x_2\right]\\
&=2b_D+(\Delta+A)(x_1+x_2)-2Ax_1x_2.
\label{eq:aggregate-welfare}
\end{align}
Define the coordinated threshold
\begin{equation}
A^P\equiv \Delta.
\label{eq:AP}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Exact coordinated optimum]
\label{prop:planner}
For $\Delta>0$ and $A>0$, the coordinated fixed-capacity optimum is characterized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $A<\Delta$, the unique optimum is $(1,1)$.
\item If $A=\Delta$, the optimum set is
\[
\{(x_1,x_2)\in[0,1]^2:x_1=1\ \text{or}\ x_2=1\}.
\]
\item If $A>\Delta$, the exact optima are $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The coordinator switches away from duplication once the complementary surplus $A$ exceeds the direct premium $\Delta$ sacrificed by assigning one region to $D$. A decentralized region contemplating that same switch captures only the downstream share $(1-\alpha)A$, so it waits until $(1-\alpha)A>\Delta$.

\begin{proposition}[Threshold ordering]
\label{prop:threshold-ordering}
For every $\Delta>0$ and $0<\alpha<1$,
\begin{equation}
A^P=\Delta
<\frac{\Delta}{1-\alpha}=A^N.
\label{eq:threshold-ordering}
\end{equation}
The threshold gap is
\begin{equation}
A^N-A^P
=\frac{\alpha\Delta}{1-\alpha}>0.
\label{eq:threshold-gap}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Priority Duplication Wedge]
\label{prop:duplication-wedge}
Suppose
\begin{equation}
\Delta<A<\frac{\Delta}{1-\alpha}.
\label{eq:wedge-region}
\end{equation}
Then $(1,1)$ is the unique decentralized Nash equilibrium, whereas the coordinated fixed-capacity planner strictly prefers $(1,0)$ or $(0,1)$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition \ref{prop:duplication-wedge} is the central result. The distortion concerns composition rather than the total amount of policy: each region has exactly the same fixed unit of policy capacity under decentralization and coordination. The two regimes differ in which activities receive that capacity. In the wedge region, decentralized governments uniquely duplicate the locally attractive activity even though reallocating one region toward the complementary activity raises aggregate surplus.

The welfare loss from duplication in the wedge region is
\begin{equation}
L
=W(1,0)-W(1,1)
=A-\Delta>0.
\label{eq:welfare-loss}
\end{equation}
Hence the cost of the coordination failure rises with $A$ within the certified intermediate region. Equation \eqref{eq:welfare-loss} is not extended beyond that region, where the decentralized equilibrium set changes.

For $A>A^N$, the differentiated equilibria $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are coordinated-optimal, but the symmetric equilibrium $(q,q)$ is not. Its welfare gap relative to a differentiated optimum can be written as
\begin{equation}
W(1,0)-W(q,q)
=A\left[(1-\alpha)(1-q)+\alpha q\right]>0.
\label{eq:highA-gap}
\end{equation}
Thus high complementarity creates efficient decentralized equilibria without making every equilibrium efficient.
